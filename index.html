    The Creed

    What every Catholic believes

    The Nicene Creed, professed at every Sunday Mass, is the Church’s own summary of the faith — the synthesis the Catechism itself expounds (CCC 26, 187). Twelve articles. Nothing here is optional, and everything the armory defends hangs off one of them. Tap any CCC reference to read the paragraph in full.

    1. I

      One God, the Father almighty

      There is one God, eternal and all-powerful, who made heaven and earth, all that is seen and unseen. Everything that exists depends on him; nothing is God but God.

      CCC 199–202, 279

    2. II

      Jesus Christ, the Only Begotten Son

      Eternally begotten of the Father before all ages: God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, of one substance with the Father. He is not a creature, and not a second god.

      CCC 242

    3. III

      The Incarnation

      For us and for our salvation, the Son came down from heaven and was made man: conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary. Jesus is one Person, true God and true man.

      CCC 456, 464, 484

    4. IV

      The Crucifixion

      For our sake he was crucified under Pontius Pilate, suffered death, and was buried. Christ died for our sins — the righteous one giving himself as the ransom for all.

      CCC 601, 619

    5. V

      The Resurrection

      On the third day he rose again, in accordance with the Scriptures. The Resurrection is the crowning truth of the faith — without it, preaching and faith are empty.

      CCC 638, 651

    6. VI

      The Ascension

      He ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of the Father — in his humanity, sharing in God’s power and authority. He has not left us: from heaven he intercedes and sends the Spirit.

      CCC 659, 663

    7. VII

      The Second Coming and the Judgment

      He will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead, and his kingdom will have no end. The resurrection of all the dead — the just and the unjust — will precede that judgment.

      CCC 673, 1038

    8. VIII

      The Holy Spirit

      The Holy Spirit is the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father and the Son, and who with the Father and the Son is adored and glorified. He spoke through the prophets, and no one can say “Jesus is Lord” except by him.

      CCC 246, 683

    9. IX

      The Church

      We believe in one, holy, catholic, and apostolic Church — the sole Church of Christ: one Lord, one faith, one Baptism, one Body given life by the one Spirit, for the sake of one hope.

      CCC 811, 866

    10. X

      One Baptism

      We confess one Baptism for the forgiveness of sins: the gateway to life in the Spirit and the door to the other sacraments. Through it we are freed from sin, reborn as sons of God, and made members of Christ.

      CCC 977, 1213

    11. XI

      The Resurrection of the Dead

      We look for the resurrection of the dead: God will raise our bodies and reunite them with our souls, glorified. Death is real, and it is not the end.

      CCC 988, 997

    12. XII

      Life Everlasting

      And the life of the world to come: the righteous will reign forever with Christ, glorified in body and soul, in a universe itself renewed. That is the end the whole Creed points to.

      CCC 1020, 1042

    Every paragraph cited here can be read in full in the Catechism reader further down the page — and every article above is defended somewhere in the deck.

    Common ground

    Three churches, one ancient faith

    The Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox churches, and the Oriental Orthodox churches all trace their bishops back to the apostles — and the Catholic Church recognizes their sacraments as valid. They are not in full communion with one another, and the differences are real. But the faith they share is older than every division between them.
    Catholic Eastern Orthodox Oriental Orthodox
    1. 1

      One Creed

      All three profess the Nicene Creed — one God, Father Almighty; Jesus Christ, “God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten, not made, consubstantial with the Father”; the Holy Spirit, “who proceeds from the Father.” The baptismal faith of the ancient Church, unchanged at its core since 381.

      Where they differ: the West added “and the Son” () to the third article; the East never accepted the addition.

    2. 2

      Seven sacraments

      Baptism, Confirmation (Chrismation), the Eucharist, Confession, Anointing of the Sick, Holy Orders, and Matrimony — all three celebrate the same seven mysteries instituted by Christ, and the Catholic Church recognizes Orthodox sacraments as valid.

      Where they differ: details vary — the East chrismates infants at baptism; the West confirms years later.

    3. 3

      Bishops in succession

      Each is led by bishops ordained in an unbroken line from the apostles, offering true sacrifice and true sacraments. This is why the Catholic Church calls them true particular Churches, not mere denominations.

      Where they differ: none accepts the pope’s universal jurisdiction. That claim is Catholic alone.

    4. 4

      The real presence

      All three believe the Eucharist is truly the Body and Blood of Christ — not a symbol, not a memorial only.

      Where they differ: the West explains it as ; the East leaves it a holy mystery. And they do not commune together — the wound is real.

    5. 5

      Mary and the saints

      All three call Mary — God-bearer — as defined at Ephesus in 431, honor her above all creatures, ask the saints’ intercession, and venerate icons.

      Where they differ: the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption as defined dogmas are Catholic; the Orthodox honor Mary’s holiness and her Dormition without defining them the same way.

    6. 6

      The councils

      Nicaea (325), Constantinople (381), Ephesus (431) — the councils that gave us the Creed and defended Christ’s divinity and Mary’s title — belong to all three.

      Where they differ: Chalcedon (451) parted East from Orient — the Oriental Orthodox reject its formula — and the later councils part Catholic from Orthodox.

    This shared faith is why the remaining differences deserve your hardest thinking — above all the papacy. See defense #52.
